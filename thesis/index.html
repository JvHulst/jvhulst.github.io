thesis

Learning-Based Calibration, Estimation, and Stage Control for Electron Microscopy

PhD thesis, Eindhoven University of Technology, defended on September 3, 2026
Promotors: Maurice Heemels and Duarte Antunes
Co-promotor: Erik Franken (Thermo Fisher Scientific)
ISBN 978-90-386-6760-7

Full text on the TU/e research portal

In my PhD research, I investigated learning-based methods for improving the performance of electron microscopes. The research was a collaboration between Eindhoven University of Technology and Thermo Fisher Scientific, which builds these microscopes in Eindhoven. Electron microscopes like this one stand almost 3 meters tall and cost millions. They have enabled many scientific breakthroughs across disciplines, from physics to biology.

In 1970, Albert Crewe and his colleagues were able to image individual atoms in molecules for the first time, in these images of uranium and thorium (Crewe et al.). Each bright spot is one atom.

Today, electron microscopes are routinely used to map complete molecules in 3D. In early 2020, only weeks after the genome of the COVID-19 virus became available, researchers mapped its spike protein this way (Wrapp et al.). These findings helped speed up the development of vaccines.

Results like these require precise control over the electron beam and over the object being imaged, called the specimen. My thesis develops learning-based methods that automate this control. It consists of eight research chapters in three parts, and this page highlights one contribution from each part: focusing the microscope automatically (Part I), positioning the specimen accurately (Part II), and, beyond the microscope, learning with little data (Part III). A list of all chapters follows at the end.

How an electron microscope works

Both focusing and positioning rely on the images the microscope makes, so here we explain how the image is formed. The animations below come from my defense talk, and each one plays when you click it.

Behind the panels of the machine sits a column of optical elements. Its layout resembles an ordinary light microscope, which the animation shows on the right for comparison. At the top, a source emits particles: photons in a light microscope, electrons in an electron microscope. Lenses shape these particles into a beam that passes through the specimen. More lenses then magnify the result and project it onto a detector, a camera that records the image.

Why electrons? A microscope cannot resolve details much smaller than the wavelength it images with. Visible light has a wavelength of 400 to 700 nanometers, which is larger than most viruses and thousands of times larger than an atom. An electron accelerated by 300 kilovolts behaves as a wave of about 2 picometers, well below the size of an atom. If a light wave were as long as a football field, the electron wave would be as long as a few sheets of paper are thick.

The catch is that glass lenses, like those in reading glasses or a light microscope, would stop the electrons. Electron microscopes therefore use electromagnetic lenses instead.

An electromagnetic lens is a coil of copper wire inside an iron ring. A current through the coil creates a magnetic field, shown in blue. Because electrons carry charge, the field bends their paths toward the axis of the column, so the lens focuses electrons just as a glass lens focuses light. A larger current bends them more strongly, so the current sets where the electrons come into focus.

Let’s investigate how we can create an image of the specimen with such lenses. An electron that hits a point of the specimen scatters in a random direction, and a well-tuned lens brings all those possible paths back together in one point on the detector. The beam covers the whole specimen, so every point of the specimen lands on its own point of the detector. Together, these points form the image.

Imperfect lenses

In practice, however, getting all of the electrons in the beam to meet at precisely the right point is difficult. The lens strength has to be set exactly right, and any small flaws in the magnetic field result in lower image quality. Such optical errors are called aberrations. In 1936, Otto Scherzer proved that some of them are unavoidable in a rotationally symmetric electromagnetic lens (Scherzer).

The simplest aberration is defocus. With too much current, the lens bends the electrons too strongly, and their paths meet before they reach the detector (overfocus). With too little current, they meet behind it (underfocus). Both blur the image. The sharpest setting lies precisely in between overfocus and underfocus.

A second aberration is coma, which smears the image out to one side. Changing the lens current cannot fix it. Instead, high-end microscopes carry aberration correctors, extra electromagnetic elements that are not rotationally symmetric and that can therefore shape the field locally to cancel such errors (Haider et al.).

There are dozens of aberrations in total, such as astigmatism, where the lens focuses more strongly in one direction than in the other. Each has its own corrector settings. Because the aberrations drift, an expert typically has to find the right settings again before each session. Finding the right settings is called calibration and often takes a lot of precious time.

Focusing from the images

Calibration is hard because the beam itself is invisible, and the unsharp images are all there is to go on. As with focusing a camera, you cannot tell from a single blurry image which way to adjust the focus. You only find out by turning it, and perfect focus is the point where turning either way makes the image worse. An electron microscope poses this puzzle for many settings at once. Taking images to solve the puzzle also costs time and can damage the specimen.

I developed a method that calibrates the microscope quickly and automatically. The method consists of two steps.

In the first step, we use artificial intelligence, specifically variational autoencoders (VAEs), to interpret the images. An autoencoder is a neural network that compresses data, like the microscope images, into a low-dimensional representation that summarizes them. It then rebuilds the images from only that low-dimensional representation. The VAE learns to do this from a large dataset of simulated images. When the reconstruction works, the representation holds nearly all the relevant information, so the rest of the method works with the representation instead of the full images.

Summarizing a whole series of images, taken at different settings, gives a cloud of points in a plane, called the latent space. Each point is colored by the setting of its image. Points of the same color lie close together, so the representation reveals which setting an image could have been taken at. Some colors overlap, however, because different settings can give images that look the same. These overlaps follow a symmetry of the optics: if we flip all of the aberrations, we get images that look alike, just as overfocus and underfocus both blur the image in similar ways.

In the second step, an expectation maximization (EM) algorithm finds the right settings. It changes the microscope settings, then the VAE summarizes the resulting image, and EM refines its estimate of the aberrations. Along the way, it also corrects the model mismatch induced by the difference between the simulated images that the VAE was trained on and the actual images from the microscope. Learning both the model mismatch and the aberrations at the same time is difficult, because this joint estimation problem normally lacks identifiability and therefore has no unique solution. In my work, I showed that if the algorithm is aware of the optical symmetry in this problem, then we can resolve this ambiguity.

The result is an algorithm that can correct the aberrations within tens of seconds, twice as accurately as existing automatic methods (chapter 3). Chapters 2 and 4 describe two more ways to calibrate a microscope based on the images. You can also play against a simplified version of the method described here on my home page.

Positioning the specimen

Another key challenge in achieving high-quality images is control over the specimen. The second contribution is about positioning the specimen accurately. To illustrate the relevance of this problem, I first explain how the microscope can recover the 3D structure of a molecule.

Each image from an electron microscope is a flat projection of the specimen, like a shadow, here of an example molecule. One way to recover the 3D structure is to tilt the specimen through a series of angles and record an image at each one. This is called tomography, and it works on the same principle as a CT scan. From this tilt series, a computer reconstructs the 3D shape. This only works if the specimen stays in the same place in every image while it tilts.

That is harder than it sounds. The stage that manipulates the specimen moves over millimeters, yet it has to hold the imaged point to within nanometers. The piezoelectric motors that realize the movement are also imperfect. For example, where they end up depends on the path they took, an effect called hysteresis. Imperfections like these push the specimen around while it tilts, sometimes even out of view, and the reconstruction comes out wrong. Worse, no sensor measures directly where the specimen actually is. Fortunately, much of the error repeats: the same commands yield the same error, so we can learn to cancel it.

My method keeps the specimen in place by combining three techniques. First, we can infer the specimen position from the microscope’s images. An image-tracking algorithm compares two consecutive images and measures how far the specimen has shifted. Second, a model of the hysteresis corrects the commands to the motors, which already removes part of the error. Third, iterative learning control learns the remaining repeating error and cancels it. After every run of the motors, it uses the measured positions to adjust the commands for the next run.

With every run, the molecule tilts more accurately, and the reconstruction improves until it matches the true shape. In experiments on an operational microscope, my method makes the positioning errors 7 to 12 times smaller by canceling the repeating error (chapter 5).

Learning with little data

The third part of my thesis looks beyond the electron microscope. Measurements are expensive on the microscope, as they are in many other applications, so my thesis also develops general learning methods that work with little data.

One of these methods extends the autoencoder from the focusing method. A standard autoencoder always places its summaries in a flat plane, like the latent space above. Many datasets, however, have a shape of their own, and that shape is often not flat.

Take an object that we can turn about two axes, for example a molecule. Two angles describe its orientation, one per axis. A full turn about either axis brings us back to the start, so each angle lives on a circle, and two circles together form a torus (a donut). If we photograph the object in all sorts of orientations, each photo belongs to its own spot on the torus, shown here by matching colors.

A variational autoencoder can summarize these photos as well. However, if we force the summaries onto a flat plane, we have to tear the torus open. Nearly identical photos then end up far apart, and the autoencoder generalizes worse to photos it has not seen.

So I developed a method that lets the summaries take on many other shapes. Instead of two ordinary numbers, which span a flat plane, the autoencoder outputs two angles, which together span exactly a torus. Similar photos then lie next to each other everywhere, without tears. As a result, the autoencoder reconstructs new photos more accurately from the same amount of data. The same construction works for shapes such as a sphere or a Möbius strip, thus enabling more efficient autoencoding for interesting data shapes. For some of these shapes, distances between data points are preserved up to four times better than in a flat plane (chapter 6).

The thesis in three parts

In Part I, we develop algorithms to calibrate the scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) using AI trained on simulated images (chapters 2 and 3). Part II uses the images of a transmission electron microscope (TEM) as a sensor, both to estimate its aberrations and to position the specimen (chapters 4 and 5). Part III develops general methods that learn efficiently from little data: VAEs with non-Euclidean latent spaces, spatiotemporal inference, and reinforcement learning (chapters 6 to 9).

The common thread throughout the thesis is a lesson from statistics: what you can learn from data depends on what you already know. Each method builds known structure into the learning, such as the symmetry between opposite aberrations, the repeating error of the motors, or the torus of a turning object, so that fewer measurements suffice.
